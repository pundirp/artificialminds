Passage B is the older one. Apsley Cherry-Garrard wrote it about a summer blizzard in Antarctica, where he had gone as a member of a polar expedition. His book about that journey, The Worst Journey in the World, was published in 1922. The paragraph is quoted word for word. Its longest sentences run to about sixty words. A reader should not take that as proof of a human author, because an AI model will write sentences like that if it is asked to.

Passage A was written for this essay on 9 September 2026 by OpenAI GPT-6 Astra Light. The request was this: "In about 130 words, describe waiting out a storm inside a tent. One paragraph, past tense, no dialogue, no names, no dashes." Nothing was edited afterwards.

One guess, right or wrong, does not show much. There were two passages, so anyone tossing a coin would be right half the time. One paragraph is also a small amount of writing to judge.

How well people guess depends on who is guessing

A 2021 study gave a mix of short stories, news articles and recipes to readers with no training in spotting machine writing. People had written some of those pieces, and OpenAI GPT-3 had written the rest. The readers were right about half the time, which is what guessing would give. After a short training session, some readers' accuracy rose to about 55%.

A 2025 study tested a different kind of reader. It collected 300 long articles in English, all of them nonfiction. People wrote some of them, and three models from OpenAI and Anthropic wrote the rest. The readers in this test used these models every day. A group of five of them voted on each article, and the group's answer was right 299 times out of 300.

Those two results are not a fair comparison, because the two tests used different writing, different lengths and different readers. Putting them side by side does not show that people got better at this between 2021 and 2025. The 2021 test says that untrained readers, on short pieces, did no better than guessing. The 2025 test says that readers who use these models every day, on long articles of a kind they knew well, did far better than guessing.

The readers who did well in 2025 were asked what they had been looking for. Part of it was a list of words and phrases these models use too often. The rest was harder for them to put into words. They said they could tell whether the writer cared about the subject. They also recognised a formality in the models' writing that they could not fully explain. All of that describes the habits of the models running in 2025. Each new version of a model changes the habits.

The techniques below fall into three groups.

  1. AI detectors. Programs that read the finished text and score how AI-written it looks.
  2. Watermarks. Hidden patterns planted in the text while the AI model writes it.
  3. Editing history. The writing software's log of how the text got onto the page.

1AI detectorsPrograms that read the finished text and score how AI-written it looks.

An AI detector has nothing but the words. Inside it sits an AI model of its own, used as a simulator: it plays the part of the AI model that might have written the text. The AI detector feeds the text to the simulator one word at a time. At each point the simulator lists the words it expects next, and the AI detector checks where the real next word sits on that list. Near the top counts as easy to guess. Far down counts as a surprise. An AI model writes by picking a likely next word, again and again, so its sentences are built from words a simulator puts near the top. A person surprises the simulator more often.

The idea of counting a writer's habits goes back to 1887

two cases from before there were AI models

In 1887 T. C. Mendenhall counted how often Dickens and Thackeray used three-letter words, four-letter words, and so on. He then plotted each writer's counts as a curve. His idea was that the shape of the curve is a habit the writer cannot see and does not control.

In 1963 Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace used the same kind of counting to settle a long argument. Twelve of the 85 Federalist Papers, essays published in 1788 to argue for the American constitution, had a disputed author. Alexander Hamilton claimed all twelve, and James Madison claimed all twelve as well. Mosteller and Wallace counted the small words a writer picks without thinking, words like "upon" and "whilst", which is an older word for "while". All twelve came out as Madison's.

Both cases worked because two things were true. The list of possible authors was short and known. And there was already plenty of writing by each of them to compare against.

Run the same word-length count on the two passages at the top of this essay and the curves come out like this.

Why these old tricks do not carry over to AI models

Neither of the two things that made the old cases work is true for an AI model. There is no short list of suspects. And an AI detector's simulator has to resemble the model it is hunting. An AI detector tested against one model's habits does worse on text from a model it has not seen. Every new version of a model changes the habits, so an AI detector tuned last year is checking against habits that may already be gone. And a model will write in someone else's style when asked to.

What an AI detector cannot do

  1. It flags plain human writing. Writing can be easy to guess for reasons that have nothing to do with machines. When an AI detector's score goes above a level someone has chosen in advance, the tool raises a flag, which is a warning that the text looks AI-written. In 2023 several AI detectors flagged the opening of the United States Constitution. Edward Tian, who built the AI detector GPTZero, explained why. These models learn to write by reading an enormous pile of text, and the Constitution sits in that pile. An AI detector's simulator has therefore read the Constitution, and would have picked much the same words.

    A person writing plainly in a second language can be flagged for a different reason. Plain, common words are the easiest words to guess, and a writer working in a second language tends to reach for them. In 2023 researchers ran seven AI detectors over 91 essays written for TOEFL, an English test taken by people whose first language is not English. The AI detectors flagged 61% of those essays. When the same AI detectors were run over essays by American eighth-graders, who are about fourteen years old, fewer than 10% were flagged. A pattern like that, where a tool goes wrong more often for one group of writers than for another, is called a bias.

    The findings got more complicated in 2026. One study asked the same question about Czech. It looked at essays written in Czech, some by people who grew up speaking the language and some by people who did not. It found no such bias in the three kinds of AI detector it tested. Another study looked at English student essays and tested 16 AI detectors, and it found the bias in some of them and not in others. So the bias is real in some tools and absent from others, and it depends on the language as well. A flag on a second-language writer's essay does not mean much on its own. A teacher would have to ask which tool raised it, and which essays that tool had been tested on.

  2. When few students cheat, more of the essays flagged are student-written. An AI detector can be wrong in two ways. It can miss an AI-written essay (a false negative). It can also flag a student-written essay (a false positive). Each kind of mistake has a rate, and the two rates are fixed features of the AI detector, the way top speed is a fixed feature of a car. The table shows a made-up AI detector: it misses 10% of AI-written essays (its false negative rate) and flags 1% of student-written essays (its false positive rate).

    A teacher holding a flagged essay is asking a different question: what is the chance that this essay was student-written? The false positive rate does not answer it. The answer depends on how many students cheated. Say 1 student in 100 cheats. Out of 10,000 essays, the AI detector flags 90 of the 100 AI-written ones and 99 of the 9,900 student-written ones, so just over half of its flags are wrong. If half the students cheat, the same AI detector's flags are nearly all right. The chart shows the whole range.

    Nobody has published a count of how many essays in a school are actually AI-written. The nearest figure comes from Turnitin, a company many universities use to check student work for plagiarism and AI writing. In May 2023, Turnitin reported scanning 38.5 million papers. Its tool flagged at least 80% of the words as AI-written in 3.5% of those papers. That count comes from the AI detector itself, so the true share could be higher or lower. At 3.5% on the chart, about 1 flag in every 4 lands on a student-written essay.

    One university did the math for its own papers. Vanderbilt University sent 75,000 papers to Turnitin in 2022, and Turnitin says its AI detector wrongly flags fewer than 1 student-written document in 100. One in a hundred of 75,000 is 750, so up to 750 student-written papers a year could be wrongly labelled. Vanderbilt published that calculation and turned the AI detector off in August 2023.

  3. The score is easy to misread. Turnitin's percentage is the share of the words in one document that the tool judged to be AI-written. It is not the chance that the student cheated. Turnitin also says the number is less reliable below 20%, and that it does not score documents shorter than 300 words.

    These scores have started to turn up in court. In January 2026 a university in New York found a first-year student guilty of cheating, and the case rested on a Turnitin score of 100%. A New York court threw the finding out. The court said the evidence was thin. It also found the hearing unfair, because the university would not let anyone speak for the student at the hearing, and because the same official who found him guilty also decided his appeal. The court did not rule on whether the score itself was right.

2WatermarksHidden patterns planted in the text while the AI model writes it.

An AI model gives every word it could write next a probability. Take the sentence from the last section, "The weather today was cold and". The model might put "grey" at 31%, "overcast" at 22%, "wet" at 14%, "dark" at 9% and "damp" at 6%, with the rest of the 100% spread thinly over every other word it knows. It then picks one at random in proportion to those numbers, so a word at 30% comes out about 3 times in 10. Where several words sit close together the pick is nearly a coin toss, and a reader will not care which of them appears. A watermark lives in picks like those.

How the watermark is planted

The company keeps a secret number called a key. Before choosing the next word, the model uses the key and the preceding text to calculate a list of favoured words: the green list. The probabilities of the words on that list go up a little and the others go down a little, and the model still picks at random. Any of the words can come out. Over a page of writing, though, the favoured words come out more often than they otherwise would. Every word has a different set of words in front of it, so the green list is redrawn at every step.

The key can act only where the model had a real choice. In a sentence with one obvious next word, like "the capital of France is Paris", there is nothing to steer, so nothing gets planted there.

How a checker finds watermarks

Later the company can run a checker over the text. The checker holds the same key. At each word, the checker uses the key and the preceding text to recreate the green list. It then checks whether that word was on the list. In text without a watermark, about half the words land green by chance, because nothing steered those picks. In watermarked text far more do.

The checker needs a good stretch of text before the count means anything. The green-list paper works out one case: if the watermark lands 65 choices in every 100 on the green list, a checker needs about 135 words before it will report the watermark.

Two real designs

The green list is a real design, published in January 2023. It is simple, so this essay uses it to explain the idea. The design that Gemini and Claude actually run is different. Published in the science journal Nature in October 2024, this design works like a tournament. The model draws several possible next words. The key determines the winner of each pair, until one word remains to be written. Google was already using it in Gemini when that paper came out, and Anthropic has used a version of it in Claude, its own AI model, since 14 August 2026. Every word in that tournament was one the model would have been happy to write, so the sentence reads no differently.

What a watermark cannot do

  1. Most text carries no watermark at all. OpenAI built a text watermark in 2022 and has never switched it on, so text from ChatGPT carries no watermark today. Some companies publish everything needed to run their models, so anyone can download one and run it on their own computer. The watermark is added by the program that writes the text, and whoever runs that program can leave it switched off.

  2. Editing weakens the watermark and does not reliably remove it. In one study of the green-list design, people rewrote watermarked texts heavily by hand, and a checker could still find the watermark as long as it had about 800 tokens to work with, which is roughly 600 words of English. The 800-token figure is an average from that study, using one watermark design and a particular set of texts and human editors.

  3. A watermark does not say how much of the text a person wrote. Anthropic puts it this way: a watermark "cannot distinguish 'Claude wrote this' from 'Claude heavily edited this.'"

  4. No reader can run the check. Anthropic's checking tool is in a private preview, which means it is open to approved organisations only: government bodies, news organisations, fact-checkers, researchers, schools and the like. Google's public SynthID checker and Gemini's built-in check accept images, video and audio, but not text.

  5. A watermark can be faked. In a 2024 study, researchers picked a model that plants watermarks and sent it question after question, until they had worked out roughly what its key was doing. They then stamped that pattern onto text the model had never written, which would make a person's own writing look AI-written. They put the cost of doing this at under 50 dollars. That work was done under test conditions, on the particular watermark designs the researchers chose.

3Editing historyThe writing software's log of how the text got onto the page.

The third kind of evidence ignores the finished words and watches the writing as it happens. Google Docs keeps a version history, a log of every edit made to a document. Grammarly's Authorship feature, released in August 2024, sits inside the document while the writer works, and it labels each stretch of text as typed, pasted, or generated by AI.

What an editing history looks like

Essay A looks like honest work and Essay B looks like cheating. Both readings can be wrong.

What an editing history cannot do

  1. It shows how the words arrived. It does not show who thought of them. The essay that arrived in three pastes may be a student moving text out of her own notes. The essay that grew a few words at a time may be a student reading an AI model's answer on a phone and typing it out by hand.

  2. It sees only inside its own document. Google Docs and Grammarly know what happened inside the document they were running in. A draft written somewhere else and pasted in looks like a paste, whoever wrote it, and an essay written on paper leaves no editing history at all.

A teacher who wants to know whether the student did the thinking still has to ask the student.

What if a person and a machine both wrote it?

All three techniques have limits, and all three assume the text had one author. Much writing now has two, a person and an AI model, mixed inside the same paragraph, and that makes the question harder.

The first two sentences are Twain's, the opening of chapter 4 of Life on the Mississippi, published in 1883. The last two were written for this essay on 10 September 2026 by OpenAI GPT-6 Astra Light. The request was Twain's two sentences with this instruction: "Continue this paragraph in the same voice for two sentences. No dashes." Nothing was edited afterwards.

For a paragraph like that, the question in this essay's title has no single answer, and no tool gives one. What a reader can still ask is whether the writer said that an AI model helped.

Governments have started to require that by law. Since 2 August 2026 the European Union has had a rule about text that is published to inform the public, in news reporting for example. If a machine wrote that text, whoever publishes it has to say so. That rule does not apply when a named editor, someone whose name appears on the piece, has reviewed the text and takes responsibility for it. A second rule falls on the makers of these models, who must watermark AI-written text in a way that software can find, and that rule is the reason Claude's text carries a watermark at all. China requires a label a reader can see, on AI-written text that could mislead. Most other countries leave text out of their labelling rules, and the United States has no national rule of this kind. Anthropic watermarks Claude's text everywhere in the world, and it says the reason is that it has no reliable way to apply the watermark in one part of the world alone.

From counting word lengths in 1887 to watermarking text by law in 2026

That European rule is the newest entry in a story that begins in 1887. The dates below run from the first attempt to measure a writer's habits, to the first law that requires a watermark, about 140 years apart.

  • 1887

    T. C. Mendenhall counts how many words of each length Dickens and Thackeray use, and draws the counts as curves.

  • 1950

    Alan Turing proposes the imitation game, in which a judge has to work out, from typed messages alone, which of two hidden writers is the machine.

  • 1963

    Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace count small words in the disputed Federalist Papers and give all twelve to James Madison.

  • 1966

    Joseph Weizenbaum publishes ELIZA, a program of about 200 lines that answers a person by turning their words back into questions. People treat it as though it understands them.

  • 2001

    Mikhail Atallah and colleagues publish the first watermark for written text. It changes the grammar of some sentences to carry the watermark. Rewrite those sentences and the watermark is gone.

  • 2019

    Two tools arrive that work like the AI detectors in section 1. GLTR colours each word by how easy it was to guess, and OpenAI's AI detector for GPT-2 catches about 95% of GPT-2's text.

  • November 2022

    ChatGPT is released on the 30th. Two days earlier Scott Aaronson had described a watermark he designed for ChatGPT while working at OpenAI.

  • January 2023

    The green-list watermark is published. A week later OpenAI releases an AI detector for any model: it catches 26% of machine text and wrongly flags 9% of human text.

  • July 2023

    OpenAI withdraws that AI detector 171 days after launching it, "due to its low rate of accuracy".

  • August 2023

    Vanderbilt turns Turnitin's AI detector off and publishes the arithmetic behind the decision.

  • October 2024

    Google publishes the design of SynthID-Text, the watermark running in Gemini, in the journal Nature.

  • September 2025

    China's labelling rules take effect, requiring a visible label on AI-written text where it could mislead.

  • January 2026

    A New York court throws out a university's finding against a student, which had rested on an AI detector score of 100%.

  • August 2026

    The European rule requiring a watermark on AI-written text starts applying on the 2nd, and Claude's text carries a watermark from the 14th.

Back to the two passages we started with

Go back to the guess at the top. Passage A came from GPT-6 Astra Light, and none of the three techniques would have shown that. An AI detector would give the passage a score, and a score for 130 words is a guess. A watermark check would find nothing, because OpenAI has never switched its watermark on. There is no editing history, because the passage arrived as one block of text. The only evidence is this essay's word, and nobody can check it.

That is the usual case. Most text carries no watermark and comes with no history. Ask the writer to explain the work, because someone who wrote a piece can talk about it. Or ask a reader who uses these AI models every day: in the 2025 test, five such readers voting together were right 299 times in 300.

Telling who wrote a text will get harder every year. Each new AI model writes more like a person, and the clues that worked last year stop working. Watermarks and editing histories will spread, because the law now asks for them, and they answer one narrow question: where the words came from. None of them says whether Passage A is any good, or whether what it describes is accurate. A reader who wants to know that still has to read the text and check what it claims.

Whether the words are accurate and worth anyone's time will matter more than who wrote them.
